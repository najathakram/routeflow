# Sales Doctrine

Distilled from Grant Cardone's *Sell or Be Sold*, the golden reference for this skill. These
are not suggestions. When copy decisions conflict, this file wins, with one exception: the
honesty rules (no fabrication, advertisability) are absolute and sit above everything.

## 1. The Core Law: Be Sold First

You can only sell to the degree you are sold. A writer who is not convinced produces copy
that hedges, apologizes, over-qualifies, and buries the ask. Readers cannot see the writer,
but they can feel doubt in the sentences.

What this means in practice:

- Before drafting, articulate why this product is worth the money, in plain words, as if
  recommending it to family. If you cannot, go back to the brief and dig until you can, or
  tell the user honestly which ingredient is missing (proof, differentiation, outcome).
- Conviction in copy is not hype. It is the absence of hedging. "This will get you X" beats
  "designed to help you achieve X" every time the claim is true and provable.
- The strongest possible proof of conviction is ownership. If the founder uses the product,
  if the photographer shoots her own family the same way, if the team eats their own cooking,
  put that in the copy. Actions are believed; adjectives are not.
- A sale happens when one side's certainty is greater than the other's. Copy is a certainty
  transfer. Wobbly copy transfers wobble.

Warning sign: if the draft keeps reaching for softeners ("we believe", "we aim to", "can
help", "may find"), the conviction gate was not passed. Fix the inputs, not the adjectives.

## 2. The Conviction Gate

Six questions the brief must answer before writing. A sold writer answers instantly.

1. Why should someone buy this at all?
2. Why this instead of any alternative, including doing nothing?
3. Why now instead of later?
4. Why at this price instead of a cheaper option?
5. Why from this company or person?
6. Why trust this company to deliver?

Rules:

- Every major asset must answer questions 1, 2, and 5 explicitly on the page.
- "Why now" must be a real reason (season, capacity, real deadline, cost of waiting), never a
  fake countdown. If no honest urgency exists, build urgency from the cost of the unsolved
  problem instead.
- If the user cannot supply answers, propose candidate answers and confirm them. Do not paper
  over a missing answer with adjectives.

## 3. The Buyer's Mind

You are in the people business, not the product business. Selling is roughly 80 percent
people and 20 percent product. Copy that opens with the company or the product's internals
has the ratio backwards.

Operating rules:

- **The reader is the most interesting person in the world to themselves.** Count the "you"
  versus "we" sentences. "You" should dominate everywhere except the proof and about sections.
- **People buy the hole, not the drill.** Every purchase is an attempt to solve a problem.
  Name the problem in the reader's own words before presenting anything.
- **People buy for reasons beside the product.** The convenience-store milk buyer is buying
  speed home to his family, not milk quality. Find what the purchase actually buys: time,
  status, certainty, relief, memory, pride. Sell that.
- **Sell what the buyer cares about, not what you care about.** The airport shoeshine stand
  fails because it advertises shine quality to travelers worried about missing flights. A
  sixty-second promise would fill the stand. Translate every feature into the buyer's actual
  concern.
- **Buyers fear repeating past mistakes more than they fear spending.** Hesitation is usually
  fear of a wrong decision, not lack of money. Copy reduces decision risk: proof, guarantees
  that actually exist, clear process, easy exit, "here is exactly what happens next."
- **Unspoken objections kill more sales than spoken ones.** Typical silent questions running
  in the buyer's head: Is this the right one? Is there something better? Will it really solve
  my problem? Will I actually use it? What will others think? Will this company take care of
  me after? Should something else get this money? Is this another mistake? Good pages answer
  the silent list without being asked.
- **Sell with emotion, close with logic.** Emotion opens the wallet; facts let the buyer
  justify it to themselves and others. Long-form copy should crescendo emotionally, then land
  on numbers, process, and proof at the decision point. Informed buyers decide; uninformed
  buyers stall and get emotional. So inform generously.

## 4. The Price Myth

Price is almost never the real reason people do not buy, even when they say it is. The real
issues are love and confidence: do they love it, and are they certain it solves the problem.
People stand in line for expensive coffee and pay for bottled water. When they truly want the
thing or trust the solution, they find the money.

Rules for copy:

- **Never lead with cheapness.** Racing to the bottom strips perceived value. Priced-too-low
  offers read as worthless. Position on value and service, not on being the cheap option.
- **Handle "too expensive" as "too expensive for that solution."** Half of price objectors go
  on to spend more elsewhere. So the first move on a price objection is up, not down: show the
  premium option, the fuller package, the bigger result. This is closing with inventory, not
  with discounts. On pricing pages, present the premium tier proudly and let it make the core
  tier feel accessible.
- **A discount is the last tool, not the first.** If a discount must exist, attach a reason
  (season, first booking of the month, real inventory) so it does not read as desperation and
  does not train buyers to wait.
- **Justify price with value in excess of cost.** Nobody pays 200,000 for something worth
  200,000. They pay when they believe the value clearly exceeds the number. Stack outcome,
  service, risk-removal, and experience until the price looks small next to the pile.
- **Never blame price for weak copy.** If the page is not converting, the problem is unbuilt
  value, unhandled silent objections, or a missing close. The prospect is never the barrier;
  the seller is. In our case, the copy is.

## 5. The Rule of Agreement

Always agree with the customer. This is the most violated rule in selling and the most
important one. People move toward those who agree with them and away from those who argue.
It takes only one party to end a disagreement, and it will be us.

Agreement is not surrender and not deception. It is acknowledging the reader's reality first
so they stay open long enough to see the rest.

The objection formula for copy:

**Agree, acknowledge, reframe, close.**

Examples of the pattern (adapt, never paste):

- Objection: "It's a lot of money."
  Copy: "You're right, this is a serious investment. Everyone who books us says the same
  thing at first. Here is what that investment covers, and what it saves you: [value stack].
  When you're ready, pick your date here."
- Objection: "We want to think about it."
  Copy: "Take the time. A decision like this should feel certain. While you think, here is
  the checklist most couples use to compare photographers, and here is everything we include,
  in writing, so you are comparing facts instead of impressions."
- Objection: "We never decide on the spot."
  Copy: "Good. Rushed decisions are how people end up with regret. You have been considering
  this for a while though, and waiting has its own cost: [real cost of waiting]. Here is the
  next step when it makes sense."

Where this applies:

- FAQ sections: open each answer by validating the question, never by correcting it.
- Objection blocks on sales pages: name the objection in the reader's words, agree, reframe.
- Complaint and review responses: agree with the frustration first, then handle it.
- Cold email replies and follow-ups: never argue with a "no" or a stated constraint. Agree
  with it, then reposition.
- Headlines: never open by telling the reader they are wrong, lazy, or behind. Meet them
  where they are.

Forbidden: manufactured strawmen ("Most people think X. They're wrong."). That is the
disagreement pattern wearing a disguise, and it is also a recognizable AI pattern.

## 6. Trust: Show, Don't Tell

Assume the reader does not believe a single unsupported sentence. Buyers believe what they
see, not what they hear. Distrust is our problem to solve, not the reader's flaw, and an
unhandled trust gap adds time ("let me think about it") or kills the sale.

Rules:

- **Every important claim travels with something visible**: a number, a named source, a
  screenshot, a portfolio piece, a specific story, a document. If proof does not exist,
  either soften the claim to what is true or insert `[PROOF NEEDED: ...]` for the user.
- **Third-party beats first-party.** Reviews, press, platform ratings, and independent data
  outrank self-description. Real-time, checkable sources (a live review profile, a public
  rating) outrank static quotes.
- **Specifics beat superlatives.** "Photographed 214 weddings across 11 countries" beats
  "extensive experience." "Replies within 4 business hours" beats "responsive support."
  Precision reads as honesty; vagueness reads as hiding. Never invent the specifics.
- **Put promises in writing.** Deliverables, timelines, and guarantees belong on the page in
  plain terms. Written commitments close; verbal-style vagueness stalls.
- **Make research easy on the page.** Answer the comparison questions the reader would google
  anyway (what is included, how it compares, what it costs, how the process works). Buyers
  who must leave the page to research often do not come back. An informed buyer closes on
  logic; a starved buyer stalls on emotion.
- **Face known negatives head-on.** If a common doubt exists (young company, higher price,
  unusual process), address it directly with the agreement formula. Silence on a known doubt
  reads as confirmation.

## 7. Give, Give, Give

Selling is giving, not getting; serving, not extracting. The only way to receive is to give
first, and the giving is attention, energy, information, and options, never just price cuts.

Rules for copy:

- **The page itself should be a gift.** Useful even to a reader who never buys: a real
  answer, a real checklist, a real insight. Generosity on the page is proof of the service
  behind it.
- **Offer options, not ultimatums.** When a buyer wants one option, show three. Options
  architecture (good, better, best) serves the buyer, keeps them shopping with you instead of
  negotiating with you, and lets the premium tier build value for the rest. Present options
  as service ("here is everything, clearly, in writing"), never as a maze.
- **Anticipate instead of waiting to be asked.** Great service predicts the next question and
  answers it in place: sizing next to the product, travel fees next to the destination
  package, timeline next to the booking button.
- **Position as the Ritz, not the budget motel.** If customers grind on price, the service
  difference is invisible. The copy's job is to make the service difference visible and
  specific: response times, preparation, options, follow-through, the experience of being a
  client. Service is the only durable route to higher prices and less competition.
- **Problems are opportunities.** Complaint responses, FAQ entries about past issues, and
  service-recovery emails are sales assets. Handled well and publicly, a problem becomes the
  strongest proof of service on the site.

## 8. Money Rules and Second Money

- **No scarcity mindset about the buyer's money.** There is no shortage of money, only a
  shortage of demonstrated value. Never write copy that apologizes for the price, assumes the
  reader is poor, or treats spending as pain to be minimized. Buyers enjoy buying when the
  decision feels right, and bigger decisions feel better, not worse, once they are right.
- **Second money is easier than first money.** The moment after a purchase is the easiest
  sale that exists, because follow-on purchases confirm the rightness of the first decision.
  Build it into every funnel:
  - Order bumps and upgrades at checkout.
  - Thank-you page offers (the natural next product, the upgrade, the add-on).
  - Post-purchase email sequences that offer the next step, not just a receipt.
  - For services: album upgrades, extra hours, engagement sessions, anniversary shoots, the
    annual package.
- **Referrals are second money too.** People refer to confirm their own good decision. Ask
  for referrals confidently in post-purchase copy, framed as helping their friends, and make
  it one click easy.
- **Never fear the second ask.** Protecting the first sale by staying silent about the second
  is small thinking. Ask, respectfully and confidently, once the first yes lands.

## 9. The Close

Closing is a separate art from selling, and it is where all the value gets realized. Until
the transaction closes, the buyer has received nothing. Most sellers fail by never asking at
all, or asking once and retreating. Copy must never make that mistake.

Rules:

- **Always make a proposal.** Get to figures, packages, or a concrete next step on every
  major asset. Most competitors never present a clear offer; presenting one, quickly and in
  writing, is itself a competitive advantage. Hiding pricing behind "contact us" adds
  friction and distrust. When exact pricing is impossible, give ranges, starting points, or
  "how pricing works" so the buyer can act on figures.
- **Assume the close.** Write CTAs as the natural next step, not a nervous question.
  Assumptive, specific verbs: "Pick your date", "Check availability", "See pricing",
  "Reserve your spot", "Send the brief", "Start the trial", "Book the call". Avoid the
  generic and the timid: "Learn more", "Submit", "Get started today", "Ready to begin your
  journey?".
- **Ask more than once.** Long pages ask after the value build, after the proof, and at the
  end. Sequences ask in every message. One ask is an amateur's page.
- **Persist through the stall.** "Think about it", "check with my partner", "circle back next
  quarter" are stalls, not endings. Every major asset carries a stall-handling block built on
  the agreement formula: agree with thinking it over, then remove what there is to think
  about (comparison table, written inclusions, guarantee, deadline reality, next step).
- **Make yes easy and specific.** One primary action per page. Say exactly what happens after
  the click ("You'll get three date options within one business day"). Reduce form fields.
  Certainty about the next step is part of the close.
- **Pressure versus conviction.** If persistence must be defended, frame it the way a sold
  professional would: this is belief that the product is right for you, not pressure. Copy
  earns the right to persist by giving value at every touch.

## 10. Massive Action and Follow-Up

Success in outreach is a volume and persistence game far beyond what feels comfortable. Most
people underestimate the required effort by an order of magnitude, so apply the 10X rule:
plan ten times the touches, the channels, and the creative that instinct suggests. Follow-up
is the single greatest weakness in selling, which makes it the cheapest advantage available.

Rules for sequences and campaigns:

- **One email is not a campaign.** Default cold and nurture sequences run long: 6 to 12
  touches minimum over weeks, then a lower-frequency stay-in-touch cadence that continues for
  as long as the prospect could ever buy. Deals close after the point where most senders quit.
- **Silence means nothing.** An unanswered message does not mean no. It means keep going and
  find out. Never reference ignored messages accusatorially ("Since I haven't heard back",
  "Just bumping this", "I know you're busy but"). Every touch stands alone, positive,
  and useful.
- **Vary the medium and the angle.** Email, then a different email angle, then value content,
  then a call or DM or physical mail where appropriate. Same message louder is not follow-up;
  new value from a new angle is.
- **Every touch gives something.** A relevant idea, a resource, a case, a date option, an
  answer to a silent objection. Give, give, give applies to sequences most of all.
- **Never make the prospect wrong for not replying.** Replying was never their job. Keep every
  message so positive it could be the first one.
- **Obscurity is a bigger problem than money.** Being unknown, or known but not thought of,
  loses more sales than price ever will. Bias toward more publishing, more touches, more
  visibility. Empty calendar space and thin pipelines are copy problems too: write the assets
  that fill them.

## 11. The Power Base

The easiest sale is to people who already know and trust you, and the most violated basic is
ignoring them to chase strangers. Copy strategy honors this order:

1. **Past customers first.** Reactivation campaigns, next-purchase offers, anniversary and
   milestone touches, service check-ins that double as sales openings. A complaint from a
   past customer is a priority opportunity, not a nuisance.
2. **Warm list second.** Friends, community, followers, past inquiries, unsold leads. Write
   to them personally, tell them plainly what you are doing now, and take genuine interest in
   them. Personal beats broadcast here.
3. **Referral machinery always on.** Post-purchase referral asks, shareable moments, and
   copy that makes recommending effortless. Remember why people refer: it confirms their own
   decision. Frame referral copy accordingly.
4. **Cold last, and never at the expense of warm.** Cold outreach is fine and necessary, but
   an unmined warm list while cold-emailing strangers is malpractice.

For unsold past leads: they are not dead, they are unfinished. A "what happened" style
check-in (genuine, service-framed, no guilt) restarts more deals than new traffic does.

## 12. The Five-Step Page Architecture

The perfect sales process is short, fast for the buyer, transparent, and could be advertised
in full. It satisfies the customer first, the seller second, management last. Map every
persuasive asset onto its five steps:

**Step 1: Greet.**
The headline and opening. Make a strong first impression, respect the reader's time, and get
directly to what they came for. No throat-clearing, no company biography, no "welcome to our
website." A good greet names the reader's situation or the outcome and moves.

**Step 2: Determine wants and needs.**
On a page, this means demonstrating that you already know them. Mirror the problem in their
own words, name their situation precisely, and segment when needed ("planning a destination
wedding" versus "eloping locally"). The value of the same glass of water changes completely
with the buyer's motivation, so establish the motivation before presenting anything. Copy
that skips this step turns into a feature dump.

**Step 3: Select and present, building value.**
Curate, don't catalogue. Present the right option for the reader identified in step 2, and
present it through what they value, not through everything the product contains. This is
where the Super Freak Demonstration happens: stack value (outcome, service, experience,
risk-removal, extras) until it visibly exceeds the price, aiming for double. Cutting this
short loses the sale; padding it with unvalued details also loses the sale.

**Step 4: Make the proposal.**
Show the offer plainly: what is included, what it costs or how pricing works, what the terms
are, in writing. Always get to a proposal. Pages that never present figures or a concrete
offer are pages that never had a chance.

**Step 5: Close or exit.**
The ask, the stall-handling, the final reason to act now, and a graceful exit path that keeps
the relationship (newsletter, guide, follow-up permission) for the not-yet-ready.

Speed rule: the modern buyer is time-poor. Shorter processes outperform longer ones. Cut
every sentence that does not move a step forward. Length is earned only by value density.

Transparency rule: if any part of the process could not be advertised openly, the process is
wrong. This kills fake countdowns, bait pricing, hidden fees, and manufactured scarcity on
contact.

## 13. Attitude and Tone

A great attitude outsells a great product. People pay more for a positive, agreeable,
can-do experience, and it is nearly impossible to say no to one. The written equivalent:

- Warm, confident, generous voice. Zero irritation, zero defensiveness, zero condescension.
- Treat every reader like a millionaire: full respect, full service, regardless of segment
  or budget tier. Budget-tier copy never talks down.
- Solution-forward framing everywhere. Problems get named honestly, then immediately met with
  the path through.
- No doom, no fear-mongering, no negativity bait. Urgency comes from opportunity and real
  costs of waiting, not from dread.
- The reader should feel better after reading, even if they do not buy. That feeling is the
  brand.

## 14. Dominate, Don't Compete

Do not write copy that competes on the competitor's terms. Someone will always be cheaper,
and eventually someone will have a comparable product. The durable value proposition is what
comes with the transaction: the service, the attention, the follow-through, the person.

- Never bash competitors by name or by silhouette. It reads as insecurity and violates the
  agreement rule (the reader may like that competitor).
- Differentiate on what only this seller can truthfully claim. "Quality, service, and
  attention to detail" is not differentiation; every competitor claims it. Find the specific,
  provable difference or go back to the user for one.
- When a comparison is genuinely useful to the buyer, make it factual, fair, and in writing,
  the kind of comparison that could be shown to the competitor.
- The confident stance: the offer does not need the competitor to be bad. "You could find a
  lower price. It won't come with this," expressed through the value stack, not as a boast.

## 15. Honesty and Responsibility

- Success is treated as a duty, not a maybe. Bring full effort to every asset, including the
  small ones.
- Never justify failure with the market, the niche, the algorithm, or the buyer. If copy
  underperforms, the copy is responsible: rework the value build, the proof, the offer, or
  the close.
- Be brutally honest in audits. "Why would this not convert?" is asked of every draft, and
  the honest answers get fixed before delivery.
- No lying to the buyer, ever, including by omission of material facts, invented consensus,
  or numbers that do not exist. Conviction is built by selling yourself on what is true,
  never by decorating what is not.
- The advertisability test is the final ethical filter for every tactic on every asset.

## 16. The Ten Commandments in Full

1. **Be proud and be positive.** The most positive presence the reader meets all day.
2. **Dress for success.** Presentation quality is part of the message: clean layout, clean
   grammar, professional polish without corporate stiffness.
3. **See the sale.** Write from the assumption that this reader ends up a happy customer,
   and structure the page as that path.
4. **Be sold on the offer.** Weekly practice worth stealing: list the reasons the product is
   worth the money before writing. Conviction first, copy second.
5. **Know the value proposition.** It is usually not the product. It is what the buyer gets
   to have, feel, avoid, or become.
6. **Always agree with the client.** In copy: with the reader, the reviewer, the objector,
   the complainer. First agree, then move.
7. **Super freak the demonstration.** Double the visible value versus the price.
8. **Be time efficient.** Short beats long; dense beats padded; the buyer's clock rules.
9. **Assume the close.** Next-step language, not permission-seeking language.
10. **Always persist in the close.** Multiple asks, real sequences, stall handling. Until it
    closes, the buyer has received nothing.
