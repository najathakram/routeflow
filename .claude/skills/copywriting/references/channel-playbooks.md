# Channel Playbooks

How the doctrine and the style rules apply per asset type. Read the section for the asset at
hand. Every playbook assumes the doctrine (`sales-doctrine.md`) and the pattern library
(`ai-style-avoidance.md`) are already in force.

## 1. Homepage

Goal: route the right visitor to the right next step in seconds, while making the value
obvious to a stranger.

Structure (five steps, compressed):
- **Hero (Greet + value in one breath).** Headline states the outcome or names the buyer's
  situation in their words. Subheadline adds the mechanism or the differentiator. Primary
  CTA immediately visible, assumptive verb. A homepage hero that describes the company
  instead of the visitor's outcome has failed.
- **Wants segmentation (Step 2).** If more than one audience arrives, split paths early
  ("Planning a full wedding" / "Eloping"), each path one click.
- **Value build (Step 3).** Three to five concrete outcomes with proof attached, not a
  feature wall. Curate.
- **Proof band.** Real numbers, real names, real work. Third-party where possible.
- **Proposal signal (Step 4).** Pricing visibility or a "how pricing works" link. Do not
  hide the money question; buyers came with it.
- **Close (Step 5).** Repeat the primary CTA with a line that says exactly what happens
  next. Graceful exit path (guide, gallery, newsletter) for the not-ready.

Channel tells to kill: rotating hero sliders of abstractions, "Welcome to", mission
paragraphs above the fold, three symmetrical icon-boxes with bold labels, "Learn More" as
the primary CTA.

## 2. Landing Page

Goal: one traffic source, one audience, one offer, one action.

Rules:
- Message match: the headline continues the exact promise of the ad or email that sent
  them. Any seam loses the click.
- One CTA, repeated. Remove navigation and every competing exit.
- Awareness calibration: problem-aware traffic needs the problem mirrored first;
  product-aware traffic can meet the offer immediately. Ask the brief which it is.
- Above the fold: headline (single most important message, outcome-focused, specific),
  subheadline (one to two sentences of clarity), CTA (action plus what they get).
- Body order by force, not convention: strongest proof early if trust is the barrier,
  price handling early if price is the known objection.
- Silent-objection block: answer the unspoken list from the doctrine before the final ask.
- Stall handling before the close: agree with thinking it over, then remove what there is
  to think about.

## 3. Pricing Page

Goal: let the buyer choose with confidence, anchored high, never apologizing for the number.

Rules:
- Present the premium option proudly and early. Move up, not down: the premium tier builds
  value for every tier below it. Never lead with the cheapest.
- Three options beat one (give, give, give). Name tiers by outcome or buyer, not by
  "Basic/Standard/Premium" defaults when better names exist.
- Under each price: the value stack, in writing, specific. The stack must visibly exceed
  the number (super freak rule) before any CTA appears.
- Show figures. If exact pricing is impossible, show starting points, ranges, or a worked
  example. "Contact us" as the only pricing information is a refusal to make a proposal.
- Handle the price objection on the page with the agreement formula ("You're right, this is
  a serious investment...") followed by value, financing or payment structure if real, and
  the cost of the cheaper path.
- Guarantee, terms, and what happens after purchase, in plain writing (trust rules).
- No fake anchor slashes, no fake "most popular" badges unless true, no countdown fictions.

## 4. Long-Form Sales Page

Goal: take a cold or problem-aware reader through the full five steps in one sitting.

Order:
1. Greet: name the situation or outcome in the reader's words; earn the scroll.
2. Problem: mirror the pain in customer language, agreement stance, no shaming.
3. Shift: the mechanism or insight that reframes the problem (why past attempts failed,
   stated without making the reader wrong).
4. Value build: outcome by outcome, each with proof, emotion rising through the section.
5. Offer: the full stack, itemized in writing, price presented after value exceeds it.
6. Risk removal: real guarantee, real process, real terms.
7. Silent objections and stalls: agreement formula, one by one.
8. Closes: ask at least three times through the back half. Vary the ask copy, not the
   destination.
9. Logic landing: near the end, the rational summary (numbers, inclusions, comparison)
   so the buyer can justify the emotional decision.
10. Final ask plus honest urgency (real capacity, real dates, real cost of waiting) and a
    P.S. that restates the single strongest reason.

Cadence: long pages need the most sentence-length variety and the least symmetric
formatting. If it reads like documentation, rewrite.

## 5. Cold Email and Outreach Sequence

Goal: start a real conversation with a stranger for a real reason.

Single-email rules:
- The reason for contact is the email. Something true and specific about them (their
  launch, their posting, their stack, their venue) connected to something you can do about
  it. If no real reason exists, do not fake one; write a straight, honest pitch instead.
- Four to seven sentences. One idea. One ask, sized small (a reply, a look, a yes/no), with
  assumptive confidence.
- No fake compliments, no assumed pain paragraphs, no "I know you're busy", no feature
  lists, no attachments on first touch.
- Subject lines: plain, specific, lowercase-normal, like a colleague wrote it. Never
  clickbait that the body cannot cash.

Sequence rules (massive action doctrine):
- Six to twelve touches over three to eight weeks, then a long-tail cadence.
- Every touch gives something new: a relevant idea, a case, a resource, an answer to a
  silent objection, a date option. Never "just following up", never "bumping this".
- Vary the angle and, where possible, the medium.
- Silence is not a no. Never reference ignored messages. Every email could be the first.
- A stated "no" gets agreement, gratitude, and a graceful long-tail slot ("makes sense;
  I'll check back next season"), not an argument.
- Breakup emails: skip the guilt-trip template ("Should I close your file?"). If ending a
  sequence, end with a gift and an open door.

## 6. Nurture and Newsletter

Goal: stay known, stay valuable, and surface buyers when they become ready. Obscurity loses
more sales than price.

Rules:
- Give-to-ask ratio heavily toward giving, but every send still contains a doorway (a
  soft proposal, a booking link, a reply prompt with a reason). Value-only sequences that
  never ask violate the close doctrine just as hard as pitch-only ones violate service.
- Write each send around one idea with provenance: something seen, learned, measured, or
  built. No consensus-restating filler issues.
- Segment by demonstrated interest when the list allows; the same glass of water has
  different value to different thirsts.
- Reactivation sends to old lists open with warmth and zero guilt, tell them plainly what
  is new, and make one easy ask (power-base doctrine).

## 7. Ad Copy (Meta, Google, and similar)

Goal: stop the right person and hand them one clear promise the landing page will keep.

Rules:
- Hook from the idea, not a template: a specific result, a specific scene, a specific
  contrast that is real. The first line does the whole job on feed placements.
- One promise per ad. The landing page must repeat it word-for-word-close.
- Proof beats adjectives even at ad length: one number or one named detail outperforms
  three superlatives.
- Honest urgency only. Platform-and-public advertisability is literal here.
- CTAs: the action itself (Check availability, See pricing, Get the guide).
- Write angles in batches around distinct buyer motivations (step 2 thinking), not five
  rewordings of one line.
- Respect platform norms without adopting platform clichés (no "POV:", no engagement
  bait, no emoji-bullet walls).

## 8. Social Captions

Goal: be known and thought of by the power base and its edges, sounding like a person.

Rules:
- Social is a party, not a podium: mostly genuine social value (the story, the detail, the
  useful thing), with who-you-are and what-you-sell woven through naturally. Direct offers
  are welcome sometimes; make them clean and confident when they appear.
- Open with the specific: the moment, the number, the line someone actually said. No
  template hooks, no "Stop scrolling."
- One idea per post. End with either a natural close (offer, link, date prompt) or simply
  end; no automatic engagement-bait question.
- Voice per platform, clichés on none. The LinkedIn wisdom shape, the broetry line breaks,
  and the "Read that again." tag are all banned.
- For client-work posts (photography, design), let the concrete detail carry the emotion.
  Name what happened, not what it "meant".

## 9. Product Descriptions

Goal: let the buyer feel the outcome and trust the specifics, fast.

Rules:
- First line: the hole, not the drill. What this makes possible or solves, for whom.
- Then the concrete: materials, dimensions, process, compatibility, care, delivery, in
  plain scannable form. Specifics are the trust engine at this length.
- Translate every feature into its consequence in the same breath ("weighted hem, so it
  hangs straight after washing").
- Anticipate the next question next to the answer (sizing beside fit, shipping beside
  price).
- One assumptive CTA. Cross-sell the natural companion (second money) after the primary
  decision, not before it.

## 10. Proposals and Quotes

Goal: a written proposal that closes, because everything important is on paper.

Rules:
- Always get to figures. A proposal without numbers is a brochure.
- Structure: their situation restated in their words (proves step 2 happened), the
  recommended option and why it fits them, the full value stack itemized, the price, the
  terms, what happens next, the ask with a signature or booking mechanism.
- Offer options (good, better, best) with a clear recommendation. Recommending is service;
  neutrality is abdication.
- Put every promise, inclusion, and timeline in writing; the document is the trust.
- Price sits after value, never on page one alone.
- End with the assumptive next step and a real validity window if one exists.

## 11. Review and Complaint Responses

Goal: turn a problem into public proof of service.

Rules:
- Agree first, always, even when the reviewer is wrong on facts: agree with the
  frustration, the experience, the expectation. Never argue publicly, never correct
  defensively, never suggest wrongdoing.
- Take it to a direct channel fast: a public reply that says, in warm plain words, that
  you saw it, you care, and you are contacting them now to fix it; then the substantive
  handling happens privately.
- Fix first, explain second, and only as much explanation as helps them.
- The public reply is written for the thousand future readers, not for the one reviewer:
  calm, specific, generous.
- Positive reviews get specific gratitude (name the detail they mentioned) and, where
  natural, a soft second-money or referral doorway.
- Know the limit: bad-faith actors who want fuel, not resolution, get one gracious reply
  and then silence.

## 12. Wedding and Creative Services

The Pixel Parchment class of work: photography, videography, design, and adjacent creative
services. Everything above applies, plus:

- **The cliché pack is radioactive here.** The wedding-lifestyle cluster (timeless,
  authentic, candid, storytelling, moments, magic) is what every competitor writes, so it
  differentiates nothing and reads as generated. Replace with gallery-level specifics:
  real venues, real weather, the thing the grandmother did, the 10:47 pm sparkler exit
  that almost didn't happen.
- **Sell the hole.** Couples are not buying photographs; they are buying the ability to be
  present at their own wedding and still keep it. They are buying what their kids will see
  in thirty years. Write to that, concretely.
- **Price handling.** Photography is a classic price-objection field. Never lead with
  discounts. Anchor with the premium collection first, stack the value (coverage hours,
  second shooter, delivery timeline, albums, the experience of being directed well), and
  handle "it's a lot of money" with the agreement formula plus the one-day,
  cannot-be-reshot reality. Payment plans if real.
- **Proof is visual and third-party.** Full real galleries (not just highlight reels),
  named reviews, publication features if real. "Award-winning" only with the award named.
- **Inquiry replies are sales pages in miniature.** Warm greet, one genuine specific about
  their inquiry, availability confirmed or held, the recommended collection with figures
  attached, what happens next, and an assumptive close ("If Saturday the 14th is your
  date, I can hold it until Friday; want me to?"). Reply speed is part of the service and
  worth stating.
- **Follow-up doctrine applies fully.** Most studios send one reply and wait. A 5 to 8
  touch inquiry sequence (gallery, planning resource, date-availability nudge, real
  booking-calendar reality) wins the ghosted inquiries without a single guilt line.
- **Second money is enormous here.** Engagement sessions, albums, parent albums, wall art,
  anniversary sessions, day-after shoots. Build the offers into proposals, galleries, and
  post-wedding sequences. The moment of gallery delivery is the easiest sale in the whole
  business.
- **Power base is the business model.** Past couples, planners, venues, and vendors refer
  more work than ads. Write the vendor-gallery delivery email, the planner thank-you, the
  anniversary check-in, and the referral ask as first-class assets, not afterthoughts.
